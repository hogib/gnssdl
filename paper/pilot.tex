\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Daily position solutions from the Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) reach formal uncertainties of about 1~mm in the horizontal components and 3--4~mm in the vertical \citep{ngl2026}. The Nevada Geodetic Laboratory (NGL) processed data from more than 17,000 GPS stations worldwide as of 2018 and distributes the resulting time series openly \citep{blewitt2018}. Using these series to study crustal deformation, such as slow slip or postseismic afterslip, first requires removing noise, part of which is shared between stations.

Common-mode error is noise shared across many stations of a network. It arises from errors in satellite orbits and clocks, in the realisation of the reference frame and from large-scale environmental loading \citep{wdowinski1997, dong2006, kreemer2021}. Several approaches have been used to estimate and remove it, from stacking \citep{wdowinski1997} and principal or independent component analysis \citep{dong2006, gruszczynski2018, zhou2022} to weighted averages and medians of neighbouring stations \citep{tianshen2016, kreemer2021}. These filters are usually evaluated by how much noise they remove, most often measured as the reduction in the root-mean-square (RMS) scatter of the filtered series \citep{tianshen2016, kreemer2021, zhou2022}.

Learned filters have been evaluated in different ways. \citet{bachelot2025} measure noise reduction as the decrease in the average position of the whole network, which should be close to zero in the absence of common-mode noise, and assess signal preservation by comparing 280 slow-slip offsets, each the difference between the mean positions in the ten days after and before an event, measured on raw and denoised series. They note that without ground truth the denoising performance cannot be quantified in an absolute sense. \citet{costantino2024} add synthetic slow-slip events to noise generated from real GNSS series by principal component analysis and Fourier phase randomisation, which preserves its spatial covariance and spectrum, and report the error of the recovered displacement as a function of the signal-to-noise ratio; on real data, they validate the result by its agreement with tectonic tremor. \citet{mastella2025}, whose method works on single stations, train on synthetic series and evaluate the noise removed from real series against the residual of a trajectory-model fit (GrAtSiD; \citealt{bedfordbevis2018}) which they treat as the reference noise, while showing the preservation of a slow-slip transient by example.

The problem with evaluating noise removed using RMS scatter is that the amount of real transient removed is not accounted for. \citet{kreemer2021} note that the main reason their filter reduces scatter more than traditional stacking is that it absorbs local common-mode transients. How much small signal survives filtering affects the science. In Cascadia, \citet{costantino2026} find that the moment--duration scaling, b-value and stress-drop distribution of slow-slip events all change with the detection threshold applied to slip inverted from denoised data, a threshold they describe as a proxy for the signal-to-noise ratio. The disagreement over a reported precursory acceleration of displacement before large earthquakes \citep{bletery2023} turned on the same question, whether a common-mode correction removes only noise or also real deformation \citep{bradley2023, bletery2025}.

\citet{bachelot2025} is the closest prior work to this study. They predict each station from its eight nearest stations beyond 400~km, and chose the 400~km empirically, as the shortest distance at which slow-slip offsets in their study area were no longer reduced by denoising. They note that this distance is specific to their region and time window, and it is not clear how either parameter should change for other networks or for transients of other sizes. Distance has been varied before: \citet{tianshen2016} estimated the common mode from stations beyond a series of increasing distance thresholds and used the change in residual scatter to infer the spatial extent of regional transients. Their thresholds served to locate transients, however, and were judged by scatter, not by how much of a transient the filter removes.

In this study we measure signal loss directly, by planting transients of known special extent, amplitude and duration in the real residuals of 1,251 stations in and around California. Each filter predicts every station from $N$ other stations, excluding all stations within a radius $R$ of it, and the prediction is subtracted as noise. Both parameters are treated as experimental variables rather than constants: $R$ varies from 0 to 400~km and $N$ from 16 stations to the whole network. We run each filter twice, once on the original residuals and once with the transients added. The difference between the two cleaned series is what remains of the planted transient, and its ratio to the transient gives the fraction of signal the filter keeps. The same runs give the noise the filter removes, as the reduction in short-period scatter, and any spurious signal it creates at nearby stations where nothing was planted. Because the transients are planted in real rather than synthetic noise, these measurements have a ground truth without depending on how well a noise model reproduces real data. Testing geodetic methods with known synthetic signals is not new: the SCEC transient-detection exercise compared detection methods on synthetic and real southern California data \citep{lohmanmurray2013}. What has not been measured is how the signal a common-mode filter removes depends on the size of the transient relative to the reach of the filter.

We apply this audit to filters from three families: target-inclusive filters, which include the target station in their own estimate (network stacking, sub-regional principal component analysis and a robust weighted median); distance-excluded filters, which exclude all stations within a fixed distance of the target, including the 400~km design of \citet{bachelot2025}; and leave-one-out filters ranging from weighted means and ridge regression to a learned kernel and a space--time attention network, the last also trained with planted transients. Realistic slip events on mapped faults and real earthquake offsets test whether the conclusions drawn from idealised transients hold for real signals. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and their processing, Section~\ref{sec:audit} the audit and its measures, and Section~\ref{sec:filters} the filters, which are listed in Table~\ref{tab:filters}. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses their implications for choosing and evaluating network filters.

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the processing chain. We take daily GNSS positions from NGL and reduce them to residual displacements by removing each station's long-term trajectory. The residuals are screened and arranged in a single array. Each filter then estimates the part of every station's residual that is shared with other stations, either from its $N$ nearest neighbours, from all stations beyond a radius $R$, or with an established common-mode method, and subtracts it. The cleaned series are scored on the noise removed, the planted transients and real offsets they keep, and the spurious signal they create. \subsection{Data}

The data are the daily IGS20 solutions of the Nevada Geodetic Laboratory \citep{ngl2026}. For each station and day, a solution gives the position of the GNSS antenna in metres relative to a fixed reference point near the station, estimated from 24 hours of satellite observations, together with formal uncertainties of about 1~mm in the horizontal and 3--4~mm in the vertical. Each station therefore provides a three-component (east, north and up) displacement series sampled once per day. In southern California the noise in these series is not only white but also temporally correlated, with flicker and random-walk components \citep{langbein2008}, which is why we plant transients in the real series rather than in synthetic noise.

We use all stations between $32.4^\circ$ and $42.1^\circ$N and between $124.6^\circ$ and $114.0^\circ$W with at least 1,095 daily solutions: 1,381 stations in California and adjacent parts of Nevada, Oregon and Baja California. We restrict the analysis to 2008 onward, when network density is between about 820 and 1,030 stations report on a typical day, and remove 130 stations: 30 with less than one year of data after 2008; 22 with non-tectonic site motion such as groundwater-driven subsidence in the Central Valley, identified by a robust scale of the trajectory-model residual (Section~\ref{sec:residuals}) above 10~mm in the horizontal or 30~mm in the vertical; and 78 co-located duplicates within 1~km of a longer-running station. The remaining 1,251 stations (Figure~\ref{fig:network}a) provide $6.45 \times 10^6$ station-days. On 1 July 2018 the median distance to the nearest station was 9.7~km, and a typical station had 22 neighbours within 50~km and 73 within 100~km.

\subsection{Residuals}
\label{sec:residuals}

Residuals are obtained by removing from each series a trajectory model of the station's long-term behaviour, fitted to that station's own data only; the residual on a given day is the difference between the observed position and this trajectory. The trajectory captures the predictable part of the motion, plate motion and seasonal cycles, so the residual retains transient deformation, whether local or regional, together with common-mode noise and the station's own noise. Plate motion is of the order of tens of millimetres per year, whereas transients are millimetres to centimetres in size; removing the known, predictable signals therefore makes the unknown transients visible.

The residuals are computed in four steps (Fig.~\ref{fig:processing}). First, for each station $i$ and component $c$, we fit by least squares the trajectory model
\begin{equation}
  x_{i,c}(t) = a + v\,(t - t_0) + \sum_{k=1}^{2}\left[s_k \sin(2\pi k t) + c_k \cos(2\pi k t)\right] + \sum_{j} b_j\, H(t - t_j) + r_{i,c}(t),
  \label{eq:trajectory}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is a constant, $v$ a linear rate, $s_k$ and $c_k$ the amplitudes of the annual ($k = 1$) and semi-annual ($k = 2$) harmonics, $b_j$ the amplitudes of Heaviside steps $H$ at the epochs $t_j$ listed in the NGL step catalogue, and $r_{i,c}(t)$ the residual, with $t$ in years. Only the training years (2008--2019) enter the fit. Second, the fitted trajectory is extrapolated to the later years, so that no validation or test day influences it. Third, steps caused by equipment changes are removed: those within the training years are estimated in the fit, and those after it with a local estimate of the jump, the median residual over the 30 days after the change minus that over the 30 days before. This local estimate uses days from the validation and test years, but no other station, and changes a series by at most a constant offset. Fourth, earthquake offsets are kept in the residuals, because they are spatially coherent deformation of the kind a filter should preserve; those within the training years are estimated in the fit, so that they do not bias the rate, and then added back.

Observations more than five robust standard deviations (1.4826 times the median absolute deviation) from a 61-day running median in any component are treated as missing, which removes 32,544 station-days, about 0.5\% of the data. Stations with unstable or non-tectonic motion are removed by the robust-scale criterion described in Section~\ref{sec:data}, applied to a trajectory fit from which earthquake offsets are also removed, so that real coseismic motion does not count against a station. Stations that develop site problems after the training years, identified by a more than threefold increase in day-to-day scatter or by a jump of more than 50~mm in the horizontal or 100~mm in the vertical with no catalogued step within 45 days, are kept as inputs but excluded from scoring; 19 stations are excluded this way.

The resulting residuals are arranged, together with their formal uncertainties and an availability mask, in an array of 1,251 stations by 6,844 days by three components. The years 2008--2019 are used for training and 2020--2021 for validation; the test years, from 2022 to 26 September 2026, are reserved for reporting, and days recorded after the method was frozen on 11 October 2026 will form a prospective test. A total of 173 stations spread across the network are withheld from all training, and the interval from 1 July 2019 to 30 June 2020 at stations within 150~km of the 2019 Ridgecrest mainshock is excluded from training, so that no model is fitted or trained on its postseismic signal.

The held-out stations are used to test how well each filter generalises to stations it has not seen in training. They are chosen by farthest-point sampling \citep{gonzalez1985}, which starts from a randomly chosen station and repeatedly adds the station farthest from all those already selected, so that the held-out stations are spread evenly across the network; 125 stations, 10\% of the network, are selected this way. A further 56 stations are held out because their records in the training years are too short for the trajectory fit, with fewer than 365 daily solutions in 2008--2019; 8 stations fall in both groups, giving 173 in total.
