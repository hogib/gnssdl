\begin{abstract}
Daily GNSS position time series are routinely filtered to suppress noise that is shared across stations, but the same filters can remove real transient deformation, because slow slip, afterslip and loading signals are spatially coherent too. Common-mode filters have been judged mainly by how much scatter they remove, a criterion that also rewards removing signal. Where signal preservation has been examined, it has been inferred from the consistency of noisy observed offsets or tested with synthetic signals in synthetic noise, and never as a function of the size of a transient relative to the reach of the filter. A recent graph-neural-network study in Cascadia showed that predicting each station only from stations more than 400~km away preserves slow slip, but chose that distance empirically for one region. Here we propose a signal-loss audit of network filtering. We plant transients of known amplitude, width and duration in the real noise of 1,251 continuous stations in and around California, and measure for each filter the fraction of each transient it retains, the spurious signal it creates at nearby stations, and the noise it removes. We audit three families under one leakage-controlled protocol: classical filters that use the target station itself (stacking, robust median imaging and regional principal component analysis), filters that exclude stations within a fixed distance (the 400~km design), and leave-one-out reconstruction models with an exclusion radius varied systematically, from weighted means to a graph-attention network. Fault-consistent transients transplanted from published source models of California events, and the 2019 Ridgecrest postseismic signal, test the conclusions on realistic signals. First results with simple filters show that using the nearest stations removes about 14\% of short-period scatter but keeps only about a quarter of a 25-km-wide transient, and that excluding stations within 50~km keeps most of it at little cost in noise removal. The audit will provide trade-off curves and practical guidance for choosing the reach of a filter, and will establish whether deep networks improve the trade-off or only the scatter.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Continuous GNSS networks record the positions of thousands of geodetic monuments every day, with formal uncertainties of about 1~mm in the horizontal and 3--5~mm in the vertical. The Nevada Geodetic Laboratory (NGL) processes more than 17,000 such stations worldwide with a single, uniform strategy and distributes the time series openly \citep{blewitt2018,ngl2026}. These records contain steady interseismic motion, coseismic offsets, postseismic relaxation, slow slip, magmatic inflation and hydrological loading, together with artefacts from antenna and receiver changes. Separating these contributions is the central task of geodetic time-series analysis.

The main obstacle is noise that is shared across stations. Errors in satellite orbits and clocks, in the realisation of the reference frame and in large-scale environmental loading produce a common-mode component of several millimetres that is coherent over hundreds to thousands of kilometres \citep{wdowinski1997,dong2006,kreemer2021}. A wide range of filters estimates and subtracts this component, from regional stacking \citep{wdowinski1997} through principal and independent component analysis \citep{dong2006,shen2014,gruszczynski2018,zhou2022,liu2026} to correlation-weighted and robust median filters \citep{tianshen2016,kreemer2021}. These filters are almost always judged by how much they reduce residual scatter or station-to-station correlation, or by how much they shrink velocity uncertainties. None of these criteria distinguishes noise from signal: a filter that also removes a real transient scores better, not worse.

This matters because the transients of interest are themselves spatially coherent. A slow slip event, afterslip or a hydrological load moves many neighbouring stations together, and a filter that estimates the common mode from those neighbours absorbs it. \citet{kreemer2021} note that much of the scatter reduction of their method comes from local common-mode transients being absorbed into the estimated common mode. The scientific consequences are measurable: in Cascadia, slow-slip scaling laws, b-values and stress drops all change with the detection threshold applied after denoising \citep{costantino2026}. The disagreement over a reported precursory acceleration before large earthquakes \citep{bletery2023} turned on the same question, whether a common-mode correction removes only noise or also real deformation \citep{bradley2023,bletery2025}.

The most direct attempt to control this loss is that of \citet{bachelot2025}. They train a graph neural network to predict each station from other stations, with the target station masked, and subtract the prediction as noise. The decisive design choice is that each station is connected only to its eight nearest stations more than 400~km away, so that the network sees the common mode but not a slow slip event under the target. The 400~km was chosen empirically, as the shortest distance at which 280 slow-slip offsets from 15 Cascadia events were no longer reduced by denoising. A simple average of the stations beyond 400~km performed about as well as the network, and better on one metric, and small offsets were slightly inflated by denoising while large ones were slightly reduced. Their work shows that excluding nearby stations is the key control on signal loss. It leaves open how the right distance depends on the size of the transient, what any filter actually loses when there is no ground truth for the offsets it is compared against, and how the result transfers to other networks and tectonic settings.

Our own preliminary analyses point to the same question. At eleven continuous stations within 80~km of the 2019 Ridgecrest sequence, every apparent anomaly before the mainshock was explained by network-wide noise, an exceptionally wet winter or equipment changes rather than by deformation on the fault. We then built a leakage-controlled benchmark on 1,251 California stations and ran the first planted-signal tests with two simple filters, a distance-weighted mean and a robust weighted median of neighbouring stations. Using the nearest stations, the weighted mean removes about 14\% of short-period scatter but keeps only 27\% of a 25-km-wide planted transient, 5\% of a 50-km-wide one and 36\% of the observed Ridgecrest postseismic displacement. Excluding stations within 50~km keeps 83\% of the 25-km-wide transient and 74\% of the Ridgecrest signal, while still removing 13\% of the scatter. Near the rupture, the filter also created spurious transients: at one station 69~km from the epicentre, the cleaned series showed an opposite-sign signal 2.4 times larger than the real one, because its neighbours closer to the rupture had moved more. The robust median kept the same amount of signal as the mean and removed less noise. These numbers were computed with idealised transients on the test years of the benchmark and will be recomputed under the final protocol (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), but they show that the trade-off is large and measurable.

Here we propose a signal-loss audit of network filtering. We test the hypothesis that the signal a filter removes is controlled mainly by the ratio of the transient's footprint to the filter's reach, and that this holds across filter families, from stacking to deep networks. The study has four objectives. First, we measure, with known transients planted in real noise, how much signal each filter retains and how much spurious signal it creates, as a function of transient width, duration and amplitude. Second, we audit the classical filters in common use and the fixed-distance design of \citet{bachelot2025} under the same protocol as leave-one-out reconstruction models with a systematically varied exclusion radius. Third, we test whether deep networks improve the trade-off between noise removed and signal kept, rather than scatter reduction alone. Fourth, we confirm the conclusions on realistic, fault-consistent transients and on the Ridgecrest postseismic signal, and translate them into guidance for choosing the reach of a filter.

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Classical common-mode filtering}

Network filtering of GNSS data began with the Southern California Integrated GPS Network, where the common-mode error was estimated as the weighted mean of the residuals at the other stations and subtracted from each series \citep{wdowinski1997}. Principal component analysis and the Karhunen--Lo\`eve expansion extended this to common modes that vary in space \citep{dong2006}. Later work made principal component filtering applicable to real networks, in which stations start and stop at different times: a modified analysis that reproduces each series from its principal components without filling gaps \citep{shen2014}, probabilistic principal component analysis that treats missing days as latent values \citep{gruszczynski2018}, and a variational Bayesian version that also selects the number of components, tested on 44 stations in southern California \citep{li2020vbpca}. Because the common mode varies in space, partitioning large networks into sub-regions improves on a single global analysis; on 208 Chinese stations, adaptive sub-regional filtering reduced residual scatter by 20--29\%, compared with 15--18\% for global principal component analysis \citep{liu2026}. Independent component analysis offers an alternative decomposition \citep{gualandi2016,zhou2022}.

\citet{tianshen2016} weighted each neighbouring station by its distance and by the correlation of its residuals with the target, chose the weights by a grid search that minimises the residual scatter after filtering, and outperformed principal component analysis by more than 13\% in scatter reduction on about 1,600 stations in western North America. They also stepped through minimum-distance thresholds to separate long-range common modes from regional ones and to estimate the spatial extent of regional transients. The common-mode component imaging of \citet{kreemer2021} takes a correlation-weighted median over nearby stations, including the target station itself when it qualifies as a filter station, and reduced residual scatter by 39--50\% on about 2,500 stations in western Europe. Across this family, performance is reported as scatter reduction, reduced inter-station correlation, velocity uncertainty or the recovery of a simulated common mode; none of these measures what the filter removes from real transients.

\subsection{Distance-excluded and learned filters}

\citet{bachelot2025} are the closest precedent for this study. Their graph neural network represents stations as nodes and connects each to its eight nearest stations beyond 400~km. During training, 30\% of the nodes are masked at random and the mean squared error is computed on the masked nodes only, so the network learns to predict a station from distant stations; the prediction is subtracted as noise. They compare it with three stack filters: the stack of \citet{wdowinski1997}, a stack that excludes stations within 50~km of tremor, and a stack of all stations beyond 400~km. On NGL data the network reduced horizontal common-mode noise by more than 70\%, and the stacks performed comparably, the 400~km stack better on the network-average metric. Training used a random 80/20 split of time windows for validation, with 2022--2023 held out for testing.

\citet{costantino2024} take a supervised route. Their network combines graph recurrent layers and transformers, learns its own adjacency between stations, and is trained to extract slow slip signals from synthetic examples: slip on the subduction interface added to noise synthesised from real Cascadia series by principal component decomposition and Fourier phase randomisation. Validated by synthetic recovery and by agreement with tremor, it was later used to invert fifteen years of denoised data for the slip history of the interface \citep{costantino2026}. Because it is taught what a slow slip event looks like, it targets signals of that form; a self-supervised filter that only predicts each station from others makes no assumption about the signal. \citet{mastella2025} model high-frequency scatter at single stations, with noise defined as the residual of a greedy trajectory decomposition \citep{bedfordbevis2018} and synthetic training noise generated by an adversarial network trained on about 5,000 NGL stations; spatial correlation is deliberately not used. \citet{teutschmann2026} pretrained a self-supervised foundation model on 17,651 NGL stations by masked latent prediction, also station by station, and fine-tuned it for displacement forecasting and the localisation of seismic offsets.

Predicting a withheld sensor from its neighbours is a familiar principle elsewhere. Tilt and compliance noise is removed from ocean-bottom seismometer records by predicting the vertical channel from the horizontal and pressure channels \citep{crawfordwebb2000,janiszewski2019}, distributed acoustic sensing records are denoised by predicting each channel from its neighbours \citep{batson2019,vandenende2023}, and graph neural networks for spatiotemporal kriging and imputation are trained by reconstructing randomly withheld sensors \citep{wu2021ignnk,cini2022grin,marisca2022spin}. Excluding nearby observations to prevent spatial autocorrelation from inflating apparent skill is likewise established in buffered spatial cross-validation \citep{lerest2014}.

\subsection{How signal preservation has been assessed}

Table~\ref{tab:assessment} summarises how the studies above test whether a filter preserves real signal. Three approaches recur. Observed transients can be compared before and after filtering, as \citet{bachelot2025} do with slow-slip offsets; this uses real signals but no ground truth, since the reference offsets are themselves noisy and the comparison cannot say how much of a signal was removed. Synthetic signals can be added to synthetic noise, as in \citet{costantino2024} and \citet{mastella2025}; this provides ground truth but depends on how realistic the synthetic noise is, and has been analysed as a function of signal-to-noise ratio rather than of spatial scale. Independent data such as tremor can confirm that extracted signals are real \citep{costantino2024}, but cannot measure what was lost. The classical filters are evaluated on noise alone.

\subsection{Transient detection with synthetic training data}

Labelled geodetic transients are scarce, so the most successful detectors are trained on synthetic signals. \citet{costantino2023} injected 60,000 synthetic slow slip events, computed with elastic dislocation models \citep{okada1992}, into surrogate noise derived from real Cascadia series, and trained a convolutional, attention-based detector on 135 stations. The detector recovered 35 of 40 events in an independent catalogue \citep{michel2019} and reached a detection threshold of about $M_w$~6.2--6.4. Other strategies include training on real labelled windows, which reached about 75\% accuracy in southwest Japan \citep{tanaka2025}, and convolutional autoencoders for InSAR time series \citep{rouetleduc2021}. Classical network-based detectors include the network strain filter \citep{ohtani2010}, Kalman smoothing with principal component analysis \citep{jiherring2013} and sparse estimation \citep{riel2014}, and the SCEC exercise compared detection methods on synthetic and real southern California data \citep{lohmanmurray2013}. In California, the only systematic statewide analysis of transient deformation is the classical study of \citet{klein2019}, which used about 1,000 stations over 1999--2018 and separated postseismic, hydrological, anthropogenic and magmatic signals. Well-documented California transients suitable as templates include the propagating shallow slow slip events of 2023 on the Superstition Hills and Imperial faults \citep{materna2024} and the afterslip of the 2010 El Mayor--Cucapah earthquake \citep{gonzalezortega2014}.

\subsection{Precursory deformation and forecasting}

The geodetic evidence for precursory deformation remains contested. The hour-scale acceleration reported by \citet{bletery2023} from 3,026 high-rate series before 90 earthquakes of $M_w \geq 7$ vanishes after common-mode correction \citep{bradley2023}, which the original authors attribute to the correction removing tectonic signal \citep{bletery2025}. Stacked tiltmeter records before the 2011 Tohoku-oki earthquake show no precursory signal above a detection limit of about $M_w$~6.4 \citep{hirose2024}, and the months-long motions reported before great subduction earthquakes \citep{bedford2020} have also been questioned. The best-supported cases are local and slow, such as the eight-month slow slip event before the 2014 Iquique earthquake \citep{socquet2017}. In earthquake forecasting, geodesy has improved skill mainly through static strain-rate maps combined with smoothed seismicity \citep{strader2018,bayona2021}; afterslip moment does not correlate with aftershock productivity across 41 earthquakes \citep{churchill2022}, neural forecasting models have not outperformed the epidemic-type aftershock sequence model in California \citep{stockman2024}, and simple models often match complex networks \citep{mignan2019,mignan2020}. Each of these questions depends on knowing what a filter does to transient signals.

\subsection{Synthesis}

Five gaps emerge. First, no study has measured with ground truth how much of a transient a common-mode filter removes as a function of the transient's spatial scale relative to the filter's reach. Second, the reach that controls signal loss has been chosen empirically, as a fixed 400~km in Cascadia \citep{bachelot2025} or by scatter-based thresholds \citep{tianshen2016}. Third, filters that use the target station, which include most classical methods, have not been compared with leave-one-out filters on signal loss. Fourth, the biases that filtering introduces have been noted only qualitatively, as the inflation of small slow-slip offsets and reduction of large ones after denoising \citep{bachelot2025}, and spurious signals created by filtering near large transients have not been quantified. Fifth, deep networks have been compared with simple filters on noise reduction, not at equal signal retention, and network-aware deep denoisers have been developed only for subduction zones.

\section{Proposed Method}

\subsection{Data}

We use NGL daily IGS20 solutions for all stations between $32.4^\circ$ and $42.1^\circ$N and between $124.6^\circ$ and $114.0^\circ$W with at least 1,095 daily solutions, which gives 1,381 stations in California and adjacent parts of Nevada, Oregon and Baja California \citep{ngl2026}. We restrict the analysis to 2008 onward, when network density is approximately constant, and remove 130 stations: 30 with less than one year of data after 2008; 22 with non-tectonic site motion such as groundwater-driven subsidence in the Central Valley, identified by a robust residual scale above 10~mm in the horizontal or 30~mm in the vertical; and 78 co-located duplicates within 1~km of a longer-running station. The remaining 1,251 stations provide $6.45 \times 10^6$ station-days. In 2018 the median distance to the nearest station was 9.7~km, and a typical station had 23 neighbours within 50~km and 75 within 100~km.

\subsection{Preprocessing and data partitioning}

For each station $i$ and component $c \in \{E, N, U\}$, we fit by least squares a trajectory model with a constant, a linear rate, annual and semi-annual harmonics and Heaviside steps at catalogued epochs,
\begin{equation}
x_{i,c}(t) = a + v\,(t - t_0) + \sum_{k=1}^{2}\left[s_k \sin(2\pi k t) + c_k \cos(2\pi k t)\right] + \sum_{j} b_j\, H(t - t_j) + r_{i,c}(t),
\end{equation}
on the training period only. We subtract the estimated steps at equipment changes but keep earthquake offsets in the residuals, because they are spatially coherent signal. Observations more than five robust standard deviations from a running median are treated as missing. The residuals $r_{i,c}(t)$, in millimetres, with their formal uncertainties and an availability mask, form an array of stations by days by components. We assign 2008--2019 to training and 2020--2021 to validation; 173 stations, spread across the network, are withheld from all training. The interval from July 2019 to June 2020 is excluded from training within 150~km of the Ridgecrest rupture, so that its postseismic signal is unseen by every model.

\subsection{Signal-loss accounting}
\label{sec:accounting}

The audit rests on four measurements, computed identically for every filter on cleaned series $r - \hat{r}$, where $\hat{r}$ is the filter's estimate of the common mode.

Retention. We plant a transient $s$ of known form in the real residuals and filter both the original and the planted data. With $\Delta = (r + s - \hat{r}_{r+s}) - (r - \hat{r}_{r})$ the change in the cleaned series caused by the plant, the retention
\begin{equation}
\rho = \frac{\langle \Delta,\, s \rangle}{\langle s,\, s \rangle}
\end{equation}
is the fraction of the transient left in the cleaned data, computed over the horizontal components, the transient's days and the stations within twice its width. Pairing the planted and unplanted runs removes the noise term that an unpaired comparison would carry; for a linear filter $\rho$ reduces exactly to $\langle s - \hat{s}, s \rangle / \langle s, s \rangle$. The first set of transients has Gaussian footprints, $s_i(t) = A \exp(-d_i^2 / 2L^2)\, g(t)\, \hat{u}$, with amplitudes $A$ of 2--10~mm, widths $L$ of 10--100~km and durations of 10--90~days: a raised-cosine rise, a 60-day hold and a symmetric fall. Fifty transients per combination, 1,800 in total, are placed at random stations and times and packed into shared runs, separated in space and time so that no station near one transient uses a neighbour near another.

Spurious signal. At stations beyond the footprint of a planted transient whose neighbours lie within it, the planted signal is zero, so any change $\Delta$ is created by the filter. We report its peak amplitude as a fraction of $A$. This quantifies the opposite-sign artefacts we observed near Ridgecrest.

Amplitude dependence. For nonlinear filters, retention can depend on amplitude. Reporting $\rho$ for each $A$ tests the observation of \citet{bachelot2025} that small offsets were inflated and large ones reduced by denoising.

Noise removed. On the test period, we compute for each station the reduction in short-period scatter, $1 - \mathrm{rms}(\mathrm{fast}(r - \hat{r})) / \mathrm{rms}(\mathrm{fast}(r))$, where the fast part is the series minus its 61-day running median, and take the median over stations.

Plotting noise removed against retention, for each filter and each exclusion radius, gives the trade-off curve that is the central result of the audit. Two further tests use realistic signals. In the event library, published source models of California transients, including the 2023 shallow slow slip events on the Superstition Hills and Imperial faults \citep{materna2024}, Ridgecrest afterslip and El Mayor--Cucapah afterslip \citep{gonzalezortega2014}, are transplanted onto other faults of the same type in the SCEC Community Fault Model \citep{plesch2024cfm}, and the displacement each real station would record is computed with triangular dislocations in an elastic half-space \citep{nikkhoo2015}. Unlike the Gaussian footprints, these reproduce the opposite motion on the two sides of a strike-slip fault, against which a neighbour average partly cancels; until they are in place, results from Gaussian transients are stated for spatially uniform transients only. Finally, we measure the fraction of the observed Ridgecrest postseismic displacement retained at stations within 80~km, on days no model was trained on, and apply the before-and-after offset comparison of \citet{bachelot2025} to the same signal for direct comparison with their metric.

The target-inclusive filters C1--C3 represent common practice: each estimates a station's common mode from data that include the station itself, so a transient local to the station can be absorbed by construction. Because they see the target, they cannot be scored on reconstruction of withheld data; they are scored only on retention, spurious signal and noise removed. D1 reproduces the 400~km stack of \citet{bachelot2025} on the California network; for their graph neural network we compare with the results they report. The leave-one-out models M1--M5 never see the target station and exclude all stations closer than $R$, which we vary from 0 to 400~km, so that $R = 400$~km reproduces the fixed distance of \citet{bachelot2025} as one point on a continuum. M1 and M2 are the simple filters of the preliminary results. M3 learns a weight for each neighbour, in a per-station variant and a variant pooled over stations with distance and azimuth as features, so that it also applies to stations absent from training. M4 lets the weights depend on frequency by regressing band-pass filtered series. M5 is a spatiotemporal transformer trained as a masked autoencoder: a convolutional patch stem embeds a 64-day window at each station, and blocks of divided space-time attention alternate temporal attention within each station with spatial attention across neighbours, biased by distance and azimuth. Its inputs include day-to-day increments, so that changes are explicit. A variant, M5-aug, adds synthetic transients to the neighbours' inputs during training while leaving the targets unchanged, to test whether a network can learn to ignore transient-shaped shared signal instead of relying on an exclusion radius.

The trainable models are fitted by minimising, over randomly withheld entries $\mathcal{M}$ of the training period,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{(i,t,c) \in \mathcal{M}} \frac{\left(r_{i,c}(t) - \hat{r}_{i,c}(t)\right)^2}{\sigma_{i,c}^2(t) + \sigma_0^2},
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_0 = 0.5$~mm. Learned models train on quiet residuals, from which the earthquake offsets estimated in the training-period fit are subtracted; windows within 30 days of catalogued earthquakes are excluded, and residuals are normalised by each station's noise level. A few stations with large coseismic offsets therefore cannot dominate the fit, and the models learn the noise rather than the coherent signal they should leave alone. The same objective drives every learned filter to predict, and therefore remove, any signal its inputs share with the target; this is why the audit measures retention directly rather than relying on the loss. A model that sees the target's own history can also extrapolate a transient's onset into withheld days, so cleaned series and all retention scores are computed with the target withheld for the whole period, and M5 is evaluated with and without its own history.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Every model is scored per station and summarised by the median over stations, so that a few stations with large earthquake offsets cannot dominate. Reconstruction errors on withheld data are split into a fast part, the error minus its 61-day running median, and a slow part, and both are normalised by the station's noise level in the training period. Stations that develop site problems after the training period, an increase in day-to-day scatter by more than a factor of three or an unexplained jump, are left out of scoring. All hyper-parameters and design decisions are made on the validation years. Because our first results were computed on 2022--2026 while the scoring rules were still being revised, those years are no longer untouched; we therefore freeze the method at a fixed date and evaluate it once on NGL data recorded after that date as a prospective test. Differences between filters are assessed with a block bootstrap over days and stations, and trainable models are run with several random seeds. We treat tectonic and other aseismic transients as signal and common-mode error and environmental loading as nuisance; daily loading products \citep{li2025loading} allow the loading contribution to the common mode to be estimated separately.

\subsection{Extension to continual transient detection}
\label{sec:continual}

The filter with the best trade-off will provide the residuals for a continual detector: a multi-scale cumulative-sum statistic combined with a spatial-coherence criterion, calibrated to a set number of false alarms per station-year and run in a strictly chronological replay with model updates restricted to windows not flagged as anomalous \citep{kirkpatrick2017}. This extension depends on the outcome of the audit.

\section{Expected Findings}

We design the study so that every outcome is informative, and we test four hypotheses.

First, we expect every filter that uses the target station or its nearest neighbours to remove most of any transient wider than the station spacing. In the preliminary results the leave-one-out weighted mean kept about a quarter of a 25-km-wide transient; target-inclusive filters such as stacking, robust median imaging and principal component analysis should keep no more, and less of transients local to a single station, while appearing to remove more noise.

Second, we expect retention to depend mainly on the ratio of transient width to exclusion radius, rising from near zero when the width exceeds the radius to near one when the radius exceeds it, while noise removal declines more slowly because the common mode is coherent over larger distances. The preliminary results follow this pattern. The 400~km design of \citet{bachelot2025} should then retain transients up to a few hundred kilometres wide but remove less local noise than intermediate radii; for California, where many transients are a few tens of kilometres wide, we expect a radius of about 25--50~km to give a better balance. Fault-consistent transients should be retained somewhat better than Gaussian ones at the same width, because neighbouring stations on opposite sides of a fault move in opposite directions.

Third, we expect filters to create spurious transients near large real signals, of opposite sign and up to the size of the real signal, at small radii, and these to disappear once the radius exceeds the signal's extent.

Fourth, we expect learned models to reduce reconstruction error relative to the weighted mean, but to improve the trade-off between noise removed and signal kept only modestly, if at all. Consistent with \citet{bachelot2025}, where a simple stack matched a graph network, a deep model that wins only on scatter would be absorbing more signal, and the audit would show it.

The main deliverables are a signal-loss table and trade-off curves for the filter families in common use, measured with ground truth on a dense strike-slip network; a quantitative basis for choosing the reach of a filter for transients of a given size; measured rates of spurious signals; and an open benchmark with data, splits, planted signals and code. Whatever the ranking of the filters, these results will let geodesists report what their filtering costs in signal alongside what it removes in noise.
