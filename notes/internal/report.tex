  \title{Noise Removal versus Signal Loss in Network Filtering of Daily GNSS Time Series: Internal Working Report}

  \author{H. O\u{g}uz Bolat}
  \ead{oguz.bolat@std.yildiz.edu.tr}
  \affiliation{organization={Department of Geomatics Engineering, Y{\i}ld{\i}z Technical University},
    city={Istanbul},

  \begin{abstract}
    This report describes, in full, a signal-loss audit of network filters for daily GNSS position time series, reports its results and discusses them. Daily IGS20 solutions of the Nevada Geodetic Laboratory for 1,251 stations in and around California are reduced to residuals by removing a trajectory model fitted on 2008--2019. Seventeen filter configurations from three families are audited: target-inclusive filters, which use the target station in its own estimate (network stack, sub-regional probabilistic PCA, robust weighted median); distance-excluded filters, which use every station beyond a radius $R$; and leave-one-out filters over nearest neighbours, from weighted means and ridge regression to a learned kernel and a space--time attention network, the last also trained with planted transients. Each filter is scored on two noise measures, the reduction in short-period scatter and the leave-one-out reconstruction error, and on two signal measures, the fraction of a planted transient that it keeps and the spurious signal it creates outside the transient. Transients are planted as idealised Gaussian fields of 10--100~km width and as fault-slip events on the SCEC Community Fault Model; real earthquake offsets and the Ridgecrest postseismic displacement are scored as well. On the test years (2022 to September 2026), the mean of every station beyond $R$ (far stack) removes 22.7\% of the short-period scatter at $R = 0$ and keeps 0.98 and 0.83 of 25- and 100-km transients; the weighted mean of the 16 nearest stations removes 14.1\% and keeps 0.25 and 0.00. The reconstruction errors of the attention network, its transient-trained version and the learned kernel over 256 neighbours are not distinguishable from that of the far stack, and each keeps less planted signal. The far stack's spurious signal is small at each station but present at every station of the network, and the velocity change of the network mean, driven by postseismic motion on the north coast, is imprinted on every cleaned series. Results on the validation years (2020--2021) are given alongside where they differ. The far stack's loss of planted signal is explained by the share of a transient that enters the network mean; nearest-neighbour filters keep fault-slip signal near the fault and remove it farther away; and scatter reduction, velocity repeatability and reconstruction error all fail to reveal these losses, so signal kept and spurious signal need to be reported with them.
  \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

GNSS stations record crustal deformation together with noise. Part of the noise is common to many stations of a network (common-mode error), and arises from errors in satellite orbits and clocks, in the realisation of the reference frame and from large-scale environmental loading \citep{wdowinski1997, dong2006, kreemer2021}. Common-mode error has been estimated by stacking \citep{wdowinski1997}, by principal or independent component analysis \citep{dong2006, gruszczynski2018, zhou2022}, and by weighted means and medians of neighbouring stations \citep{tianshen2016, kreemer2021}. The evaluation reported for these filters is most often the reduction in the root-mean-square (RMS) scatter of the filtered series \citep{tianshen2016, kreemer2021, zhou2022}. \citet{kreemer2021} note that the main reason their filter reduces scatter more than traditional stacking is that it absorbs local common-mode transients.

Learned filters have been evaluated in other ways. \citet{bachelot2025} predict each station from its eight nearest stations beyond 400~km, a distance chosen empirically in their study area as the shortest at which slow-slip offsets were no longer reduced by denoising, and they assess signal preservation on 280 slow-slip offsets measured on raw and denoised series. \citet{costantino2024} add synthetic slow-slip events to noise generated from real GNSS series and report the error of the recovered displacement against the signal-to-noise ratio. \citet{mastella2025} train a single-station method on synthetic series and evaluate the noise removed from real series against the residual of a trajectory-model fit \citep{bedfordbevis2018}. \citet{tianshen2016} estimated the common mode from stations beyond increasing distance thresholds and used the threshold at which the RMS changed most sharply as the approximate extent of a regional transient. \citet{gualandi2016} evaluated source separation on simulated networks with known sources in synthetic noise. In Cascadia, \citet{costantino2026} report that the scaling, b-value and stress-drop distribution of slow-slip events change with the detection threshold applied to slip inverted from denoised data, and the debate over a reported precursory acceleration of slip \citep{bletery2023, bletery2025} turned on whether a common-mode correction removes only noise.

The study described here measures signal loss directly. Transients of known extent, amplitude and duration are planted in the real residuals of 1,251 stations; each filter is run on the residuals with and without them; and the difference between the two cleaned series gives the fraction of each transient that survives. The same runs give the noise each filter removes and the spurious signal it creates where nothing was planted. Each filter predicts a station from $N$ other stations, excluding every station within a radius $R$ of it; both $N$ (from 16 stations to the whole network) and $R$ (from 0 to 600~km) are treated as experimental variables. Section~\ref{sec:literature} reviews the literature, Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and their processing, Section~\ref{sec:audit} the audit, Section~\ref{sec:filters} the filters (Table~\ref{tab:filters}), Section~\ref{sec:results} the results, Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses them and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.

\section{Literature review}
\label{sec:literature}
%% ===========================================================================

This section is condensed from the research proposal. Two works cited there are left out: a blog post, and a study the author has not read in full.

\subsection{Classical common-mode filtering}

Network filtering of GNSS data began with the Southern California Integrated GPS Network, where the common-mode error was estimated as the weighted mean of the residuals at the other stations and subtracted from each series \citep{wdowinski1997}. Principal component analysis and the Karhunen--Lo\`eve expansion extended this to common modes that vary in space \citep{dong2006}. Later work made principal component filtering applicable to networks whose stations start and stop at different times: a modified analysis that reproduces each series from its principal components without filling gaps \citep{shen2014}, probabilistic principal component analysis that treats missing days as latent values \citep{gruszczynski2018}, and a variational Bayesian version that also selects the number of components \citep{li2020vbpca}. Because the common mode varies in space, large networks have been divided into sub-regions; on 208 Chinese stations, adaptive sub-regional filtering reduced residual scatter by 20--29\%, against 15--18\% for a single global analysis \citep{liu2026}. Independent component analysis offers an alternative decomposition \citep{gualandi2016, zhou2022}.

\citet{tianshen2016} weighted each neighbouring station by its distance and by the correlation of its residuals with the target, chose the weights by a grid search that minimises the residual scatter after filtering, and outperformed principal component analysis by more than 13\% in scatter reduction on about 1,600 stations in western North America. They also stepped through minimum-distance thresholds to separate long-range from regional common modes and to estimate the extent of regional transients. The common-mode component imaging of \citet{kreemer2021} takes a correlation-weighted median over nearby stations, including the target itself when it qualifies, and reduced residual scatter by 39--50\% on about 2,500 stations in western Europe. Across this family, performance is reported as scatter reduction, reduced inter-station correlation, velocity uncertainty or the recovery of a simulated common mode.

\subsection{Distance-excluded and learned filters}

The graph neural network of \citet{bachelot2025} represents stations as nodes and connects each to its eight nearest stations beyond 400~km. During training, 30\% of the nodes are masked and the error is computed on the masked nodes only, so the network learns to predict a station from distant stations, and the prediction is subtracted as noise. They compared it with three stacks, including a stack of all stations beyond 400~km, which performed comparably and better on the network-average metric. The 400~km was chosen as the shortest distance at which 280 slow-slip offsets from 15 Cascadia events were no longer reduced by denoising.

\citet{costantino2024} train a network of graph recurrent layers and transformers to extract slow slip from synthetic examples, slip on the subduction interface added to noise synthesised from real Cascadia series by principal component decomposition and Fourier phase randomisation; validated by synthetic recovery and agreement with tremor, it was later used to invert fifteen years of denoised data \citep{costantino2026}. \citet{mastella2025} model high-frequency scatter at single stations, with noise defined as the residual of a trajectory decomposition \citep{bedfordbevis2018} and synthetic training noise from an adversarial network. \citet{teutschmann2026} pretrained a self-supervised foundation model on 17,651 NGL stations by masked latent prediction, station by station, and fine-tuned it for displacement forecasting and the localisation of seismic offsets.

Predicting a withheld sensor from its neighbours is established elsewhere: tilt and compliance noise is removed from ocean-bottom seismometers by predicting the vertical channel from other channels \citep{crawfordwebb2000, janiszewski2019}, distributed acoustic sensing records are denoised by predicting each channel from its neighbours \citep{batson2019, vandenende2023}, and graph networks for spatiotemporal kriging are trained by reconstructing withheld sensors \citep{wu2021ignnk, cini2022grin, marisca2022spin}. Excluding nearby observations so that spatial autocorrelation does not inflate apparent skill is the basis of buffered spatial cross-validation \citep{lerest2014}.

\subsection{How signal preservation has been assessed}

Three approaches recur (Table~\ref{tab:assessment}). Observed transients can be compared before and after filtering \citep{bachelot2025}; this uses real signals but has no ground truth. Synthetic signals can be added to synthetic noise \citep{costantino2024, mastella2025}; this has ground truth but depends on how realistic the noise is, and has been analysed against signal-to-noise ratio rather than spatial scale. Independent data such as tremor can confirm that an extracted signal is real \citep{costantino2024}, but not measure what was lost. Detectors of transients are commonly trained on synthetic signals: \citet{costantino2023} injected 60,000 synthetic slow-slip events computed with elastic dislocations \citep{okada1992} into surrogate noise and recovered 35 of 40 catalogued events \citep{michel2019}; other approaches include training on real labelled windows \citep{tanaka2025}, autoencoders for InSAR time series \citep{rouetleduc2021}, the network strain filter \citep{ohtani2010}, Kalman smoothing with principal components \citep{jiherring2013} and sparse estimation \citep{riel2014}. The only statewide analysis of transient deformation in California is that of \citet{klein2019}.

\subsection{Gaps}

Four gaps motivate the study. No study has measured, with ground truth, how much of a transient a common-mode filter removes as a function of the transient's size relative to the filter's reach. The reach has been chosen empirically, as a fixed 400~km \citep{bachelot2025} or by scatter-based thresholds \citep{tianshen2016}. Filters that use the target station, which include most classical methods, have not been compared with leave-one-out filters on signal loss. And learned filters have been compared with simple ones on noise reduction, not at equal signal kept.

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:stations}

The data are the daily IGS20 solutions of the Nevada Geodetic Laboratory (NGL) \citep{blewitt2018, ngl2026}. Each solution gives the east, north and up position of a station's antenna relative to a fixed reference point near the station, estimated from 24~hours of observations, with formal uncertainties of about 1~mm in the horizontal and 3--4~mm in the vertical. NGL publishes new days with a latency of about ten days. In southern California the noise in these series contains flicker and random-walk components in addition to white noise \citep{langbein2008}.

All stations between $32.4^\circ$ and $42.1^\circ$N and between $124.6^\circ$ and $114.0^\circ$W with at least 1,095 daily solutions were taken: 1,381 stations in California and adjacent parts of Nevada, Oregon and Baja California. The analysis starts on 1 January 2008. About 390 stations reported on a typical day in 2005, and between about 820 and 1,030 have done so since 2008. Of the 1,381 stations, 130 were removed: 30 with less than one year of data after 2008; 22 with non-tectonic site motion, such as groundwater-driven subsidence in the Central Valley, identified by a robust scale of the trajectory residual (Section~\ref{sec:residuals}) above 10~mm in the horizontal or 30~mm in the vertical; and 78 co-located duplicates within 1~km of a longer-running station, of which only the station with the most data was kept. The remaining 1,251 stations (Fig.~\ref{fig:network}) give $6.45 \times 10^6$ station-days. On 1 July 2018 the median distance to the nearest station was 9.7~km, and a typical station had 22 stations within 50~km and 73 within 100~km. The 16th-nearest neighbour of a station lies at a median distance of 40~km (10th--90th percentiles 22--79~km), the 64th at 88~km and the 256th at 204~km.

\subsection{Residuals}
\label{sec:residuals}

For each station $i$ and component $c$, the trajectory model
\begin{equation}
  x_{i,c}(t) = a + v\,(t - t_0) + \sum_{k=1}^{2}\left[p_k \sin(2\pi k t) + q_k \cos(2\pi k t)\right] + \sum_{j} b_j\, H(t - t_j) + r_{i,c}(t)
  \label{eq:trajectory}
\end{equation}
is fitted by least squares, where $a$ is a constant, $v$ a linear rate about the mean epoch $t_0$ of the fitted data, $p_k$ and $q_k$ the amplitudes of the annual and semi-annual harmonics, $b_j$ the amplitudes of Heaviside steps $H$ at the epochs $t_j$ of the NGL step catalogue, $r_{i,c}(t)$ the residual and $t$ the time in years (Fig.~\ref{fig:processing}). Only training days (2008--2019, outside the Ridgecrest exclusion described below) enter the fit, and the fitted trajectory is extrapolated to later years. Equipment steps are removed everywhere: within the training years they are estimated in the fit, and after them by a local estimate, the median residual over the 30 days after the change minus that over the 30 days before. Earthquake steps are not removed from the residuals; those within the training years are estimated in the fit, so that they do not bias the rate, and then added back. A station with fewer than 365 training epochs is fitted on its first 730 days, or its first 365 epochs, whichever reaches further, and is held out (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}).

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{data_processing.pdf}
  \caption{From positions to residuals at station BSRY: (a) positions and fitted trajectory, (b) residual, (c) one validation year with the network common mode and the cleaned series.}
  \label{fig:processing}
\end{figure}

A value more than five robust standard deviations (1.4826 times the median absolute deviation) from its 61-day running median in any component is treated as missing; 32,544 station-days, about 0.5\% of the data, are removed this way. The station QC of Section~\ref{sec:stations} uses a second trajectory fit from which earthquake steps are also removed, so that coseismic motion does not count against a station. Stations that develop problems after the training years are kept as inputs but not scored: those whose day-to-day scatter increases more than threefold, and those with a jump of more than 50~mm in the horizontal or 100~mm in the vertical between consecutive 30-day medians with no catalogued step within 45 days; 19 stations are excluded from scoring this way. The local equipment-step estimate and the outlier screening use days from every year, including the validation and test years, but no other station; their effect on a day that is kept is at most a constant shift or the removal of a spike.

The residuals, their formal uncertainties and an availability mask are stored in an array of 1,251 stations by 6,844 days by three components, together with the neighbour lists of every station at each exclusion radius (the 16 nearest stations beyond $R$, searched to $R + 300$~km). Days are split into training (2008--2019), validation (2020--2021) and test years (2022 to 26 September 2026, the last day available when the method was frozen); days published after the freeze will form a prospective test.

\subsection{Held-out stations and excluded intervals}
\label{sec:heldout}

The held-out stations are never used in training, and serve to test how well trained filters generalise to stations they have not seen. 125 stations, 10\% of the network, are chosen by farthest-point sampling \citep{gonzalez1985} with seed 0, which starts from a random station and repeatedly adds the station farthest from all those already chosen. A further 56 stations are held out because they have fewer than 365 daily solutions in 2008--2019; 8 stations are in both groups, giving 173 held-out stations. The trajectories of the 56 short-record stations are fitted on their first 730 days, which can extend into the validation or test years, so their residuals are detrended on data they are later scored on; this affects the slow part of their series, which is not scored, and not the fast band. The interval from 1 July 2019 to 30 June 2020 at stations within 150~km of the 2019 Ridgecrest mainshock ($35.77^\circ$N, $117.60^\circ$W) is excluded from training, so that no model is fitted or trained on its postseismic signal.

\subsection{Displacement rather than increments}

Filters are applied to displacement rather than to daily increments. For example, 2~cm of postseismic displacement accumulated over a year is a daily increment of about 0.055~mm, whereas the day-to-day scatter of the increments is about 1.4~mm in the horizontal components. Increments are used only as an additional input to the attention network (Section~\ref{sec:filters-learned}).

\subsection{Leave-one-out filtering and the exclusion radius}
\label{sec:loo}

A filter $P$ estimates the part of the residual of station $i$ that is shared with other stations, and the cleaned series is $r - P(r)$. A leave-one-out filter never reads station $i$ itself when estimating it, and never reads any station within $R$~km of it (Fig.~\ref{fig:radius}). The target-inclusive filters of Section~\ref{sec:filters-inclusive} read the target on purpose, as published filters of their kind do; they are applied to the same cube and scored on noise removed and on the signal measures, but not on reconstruction error. The exclusion radius $R$ is an experimental variable: M1 is run at $R = 0$, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200 and 400~km and the far stack at 0, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400 and 600~km; the other leave-one-out filters are run at the radii in Table~\ref{tab:radii}, and M2, M3 and M4 at $R = 0$ only.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{network_radius.pdf}
  \caption{What a prediction for one target may use at $R = 50$~km.}
  \label{fig:radius}
\end{figure}

Two leak checks are run for every leave-one-out filter at every radius, on the first year of the validation period. Both compare predictions on two inputs that differ only in values the filter must not read. In the radius check, the target and every station within $R$ of it are set to a poison value of $10^6$~mm, for five randomly chosen targets. In the mask check, the cells hidden for the mask scores below are filled once with missing values and once with the poison value, while remaining marked as unavailable. In both, the predictions must agree to within 0.001~mm. Every filter passed both checks at every radius with a largest difference of 0.0~mm. Because a filter that passes the radius check never reads the target, one prediction on the unhidden cube is a leave-one-out prediction for every station at once, and this is how all cleaned series are produced.

The harness also computes scores on fixed evaluation masks (seed 20260101): 10\% of available station-days at random (\texttt{scatter}), one 30-day block per station and year (\texttt{block}), all days of the held-out stations (\texttt{station}) and the Ridgecrest window. These mask scores are not the reconstruction error of Section~\ref{sec:measures}; they are used here only for the spread between training seeds (Section~\ref{sec:res-learned}), and are labelled as such.

\subsection{Measures of noise removed and of planted transients}
\label{sec:measures}

Filters are compared on two measures of noise and two of signal, with a third, velocity repeatability, reported separately. Noise removed is the reduction in short-period scatter at each scored station,
\begin{equation}
  1 - \frac{\mathrm{rms}\big(\mathrm{fast}(r - P(r))\big)}{\mathrm{rms}\big(\mathrm{fast}(r)\big)},
  \label{eq:noise}
\end{equation}
where the fast part of a series is the series minus its 61-day running median. It is averaged over the three components and summarised by its median over stations. Predicting zero gives 0. The measure covers the fast band only. Common-mode error at periods longer than about two months, which stacking and principal component filters also remove and which matters for velocities and strain rates, earns no credit here; velocity repeatability is the only long-period measure in the audit, and every statement below about noise removed is a statement about the fast band.

Reconstruction error is the root mean square of the fast part of the leave-one-out error $r - P(r)$, normalised by each station's robust noise scale from the training years, averaged over the three components, and summarised by its median over the seen, scored stations. Days within $\pm 30$ days of an M $\ge$ 6 earthquake catalogued for a station are left out. Predicting zero gives 0.753 on the test years and 0.723 on the validation years rather than 1, because separating the slow part also removes some of each station's own noise. Reconstruction error is the criterion on which the leave-one-out filters were tuned, on the validation years. The error is lower when the prediction reproduces whatever the target shares with other stations, signal included, so tuning on it favours settings that absorb coherent signal in the fast band. The signal measures below are therefore not refinements of the tuning criterion but independent checks on what it selected.

Retention measures the fraction of a planted transient that survives filtering. A transient $s$ is added to the residuals, and both the original and the modified residuals are filtered. The change in the cleaned series and the retention are
\begin{equation}
  \Delta = \big(r + s - P(r+s)\big) - \big(r - P(r)\big),
  \qquad
  \rho = \frac{\langle \Delta,\, s \rangle}{\langle s,\, s \rangle},
  \label{eq:retention}
\end{equation}
with the inner products over the horizontal components, the days of the transient and the scored stations within $2L$ of its centre, where $L$ is its width. Pairing with the unmodified run removes the noise term; for a linear filter, $\rho = \langle s - P(s), s\rangle / \langle s, s \rangle$ exactly. $\rho = 1$ means that the transient is kept, $\rho = 0$ that it is removed, and $\rho > 1$ that the filter adds to it. Retention is summarised by its median over transients.

Spurious signal is the change created where nothing was planted. A Gaussian transient is set to zero beyond $3L$ from its centre (its footprint). At the scored stations outside the footprint whose cleaned series the transient can change, which depend on the filter (stations with a neighbour inside the footprint for the nearest-neighbour filters, stations whose far set includes one for the far stack, every station of the same region for C3, and every station for C1), the peak horizontal amplitude of $\Delta$ is taken as a fraction of the amplitude of the transient, the largest value over these stations is kept, and its median over transients is reported. For fault-slip events, which have no single amplitude, the stations outside are those moved by less than 0.1~mm by the event, and the reference is the event's largest displacement at a scored station. The largest value does not show how many stations are affected, which differs by two orders of magnitude between filters: a few dozen for the nearest-neighbour filters, most of the network for the far stack and C1. The number of affected stations and the sum over them of the peak spurious change, in units of the planted amplitude, are therefore also reported (Section~\ref{sec:res-grid}); both are computed from quantities recorded with every injection, the sum as a summary added on 30 October 2026.

Velocity repeatability compares the velocity of each cleaned series on 2022--2023 with that on 2024--2025. Each velocity is a MIDAS-style estimate \citep{blewitt2016}: the median of the slopes between all pairs of days exactly 365 days apart within the window, recomputed after removing slopes more than two robust standard deviations from the first median. Pairs that span a catalogued earthquake step or touch a day left out of scoring are skipped, and at least 100 pairs are required. The measure is the median over stations of $|\Delta v|$, the absolute difference between the two velocities, per component. It was defined before the freeze and implemented on 12 October 2026. Because the subtracted series is the same, or nearly the same, at every station for a network-wide filter, the velocity change of the common-mode estimate itself is imprinted with opposite sign on every cleaned series; Section~\ref{sec:res-velocity} measures it.

Seasonal retention (added 30 October 2026) measures what a filter would do to a station's seasonal signal, which the trajectory fit removes before any filter runs. Each station's fitted annual and semi-annual harmonic, $p_1 \sin 2\pi t + q_1 \cos 2\pi t + p_2 \sin 4\pi t + q_2 \cos 4\pi t$ with $t$ in years, is added back to its residuals over the period, every station at once as in the real series; the filter is run with and without it; and the paired retention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:retention} is computed per station and component over the available days of the period. $\rho = 1$ means the filter would leave the station's seasonal signal alone, $\rho = 0$ that it would remove it. For a linear filter the result does not depend on the residuals. A network-wide filter removes the part of the seasonal signal shared by the stations it averages and keeps the station-specific part; the measure reports how large that part is. The seasonal signal is nuisance in this audit (Section~\ref{sec:intro}), but the removed harmonic is a deterministic function that can be added back to any cleaned series, and a user who keeps it needs to know what the filter would have done to it.

\subsection{Planted and real signals}
\label{sec:planted}

Gaussian transients have the form
\begin{equation}
  s(d, t) = A \exp\!\left(-\frac{d^2}{2L^2}\right) g(t)\, \mathbf{u},
  \label{eq:gaussian}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the distance of a station from the centre, $A$ the amplitude, $L$ the width, $\mathbf{u}$ a horizontal unit vector in a random direction and $g(t)$ the time profile: a raised-cosine rise over $D$ days, a 60-day hold and a raised-cosine fall over $D$ days, after which the transient is zero, so it leaves no permanent offset. $s$ is zero beyond $3L$, and every station in the footprint moves in the same direction. The grid is $A \in \{2, 5, 10\}$~mm, $L \in \{10, 25, 50, 100\}$~km and $D \in \{10, 30, 90\}$~days. Each of the 36 combinations is planted at 20 centres, each at a randomly chosen scored station, giving 720 transients per filter and radius, of which 714--719 have at least one scored station within $2L$. Onsets fall in 300-day slots of the scored period, and the signal is added only on days with data. Many transients share one run of a filter: two share a run only if no station's cleaned series can be changed by both, so the $\Delta$ at every station comes from one transient, and fewer transients fit in a run at larger radii.

Fault-slip events are computed from source models taken from published studies and moved to other faults of the same style in the SCEC Community Fault Model 7.0 \citep{plesch2007, plesch2024cfm}. Fault surfaces (UTM zone 11, NAD27) are triangulated; a patch of the template's length and depth range is placed at a random position along a fault of the same style, with dip within $\pm 15^\circ$ of the template's; slip is tapered to zero over the outer 20\% of the patch (not at the top when it reaches the surface); and east, north and up displacements at every station are computed from triangular dislocations in an elastic half-space with a Poisson's ratio of 0.25 \citep{nikkhoo2015}. Every template value is a range from the full text of its source, drawn per event. Shallow slow slip follows the 2023 Superstition Hills and Imperial fault events \citep{materna2024, vavra2024}: 8--25~km long, from the surface to 2--4~km depth, 8--45~mm of slip, a slip front moving at 0.4--9~km/day with a local rise of 1--8 days. Afterslip follows the 2010 El Mayor--Cucapah earthquake \citep{gonzalezortega2014}: 15--50~km long, from 0--2 to 5--12~km depth, 10--40~cm of slip with a logarithmic time function ($\tau = 17$--34 days), followed for 365 days. Slip is permanent after the event. An event is kept only where at least three scored stations move by more than 1~mm, and two events share a run only if no station moves by more than 0.1~mm in both. Twenty events of each template are planted, the same for every filter. Retention is computed as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:retention} over the stations moving by more than 1~mm, and also per station, with the station's distance from the slipping patch and its side of the fault. On the test years the afterslip events move a median of 28.5 scored stations by more than 1~mm, with a median largest displacement of 14.3~mm; the shallow slow-slip events move a median of 3.5 stations, with 4.6~mm. A third template, shallow creep triggered on a neighbouring fault \citep{ramos2020, xu2020}, was dropped because only 1 of 300 random placements moved three or more scored stations by more than 1~mm. The elastic half-space, uniform tapered slip, afterslip without its earthquake and placement on any fault of the right style are the limits of this test.

Real earthquake offsets are every catalogued earthquake step in the scored period at a scored station, with an observed horizontal offset of at least 3~mm and no other catalogued step within 15 days. The offset is the mean position over the 10 days after the earthquake minus that over the 10 days before (at least 5 available days on each side), and the fraction kept is $\langle \text{kept}, \text{observed} \rangle / \langle \text{observed}, \text{observed} \rangle$, with ``kept'' the same difference measured on the cleaned series. On the test years there are 81 such offsets (median 6.8~mm): 54 from the 5 December 2024 M7.0 Cape Mendocino earthquake, 18 from the 20 December 2022 M6.4 Ferndale earthquake, 5 from an M5.6 event on 24 June 2026 and 2 each from two M5.7 events (9 December 2024 and 14 April 2026; catalogue identifiers nn00888580 and nn00914068). These offsets have no ground truth, since the observed offset includes noise.

The Ridgecrest test uses the real postseismic displacement after the 6 July 2019 mainshock: at the 182 scored stations within 80~km, the horizontal displacement over days 7--365 relative to the mean of days 1--6 is compared with the same quantity in the cleaned series, as $\langle \text{kept}, \text{observed} \rangle / \langle \text{observed}, \text{observed} \rangle$ per station, and the median over stations is reported. These days are excluded from training, and 2019 is in neither the validation nor the test years, so the result is the same for every period. \subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

All design decisions, hyper-parameters and model choices were made on the validation years, and the results below are from the test years unless marked otherwise. The method was frozen on 11 October 2026 (repository tag \texttt{freeze-2026-10-11}) with the intention of reporting the test years untouched. That intention did not hold: the test years had been looked at while the scoring rules were revised on 7 October 2026; velocity repeatability, the bootstrap split by seen and held-out stations, the spurious extent and the seasonal retention measure were added after the freeze; and a further deep model was built after it. On 30 October 2026 the freeze was therefore lifted and the whole California data set, all years and all stations, declared development data. The split names are kept for the harness and the tables, but no claim in this report rests on the test years being untouched. The held-out evaluation will be a different network, chosen and processed once the method is settled, on which every filter is scored once with the settings chosen on California; a prospective test on California (days after 11 October 2026) remains available as a secondary check.

Differences in reconstruction error are tested with a paired block bootstrap (\texttt{gnssdl bench compare}): each station's leave-one-out fast error is summarised per 30-day block, and blocks (the same for every station) and stations are resampled 1,000 times to give a 95\% interval for the difference in the median, run separately for seen stations, held-out stations and all stations. Differences in retention are tested by resampling the planted transients, paired by identifier, for each width. A difference whose interval includes zero is reported as not distinguishable. Trained filters were run with five seeds.

\subsection{Target-inclusive filters}
\label{sec:filters-inclusive}

C1 is the stack of \citet{wdowinski1997}: the common mode of every station on a day is the unweighted mean of all available stations that day, the station itself included. C3 is sub-regional probabilistic PCA \citep{tipping1999}, applied as in \citet{gruszczynski2018} with regions as in \citet{liu2026}: stations are grouped into 1, 4 or 8 regions by k-means on their projected coordinates; in each region and component, a probabilistic PCA with 1, 2 or 3 components is fitted by expectation--maximisation on the quiet training residuals (Section~\ref{sec:filters-learned}), normalised per station, with missing days treated as latent; on any day the component scores are estimated from every available station of the region, the target included, and the common mode is the reconstruction. The nine settings are reported as a range, and individually in Table~\ref{tab:c3}. M2-self is the robust weighted median of M2 (Section~\ref{sec:filters-loo}) with the target's own value added to the median, as in CMC Imaging \citep{tianshen2016, kreemer2021}; it reuses M2's tuned settings.

\subsection{Distance-excluded filters}
\label{sec:filters-distance}

The far stack is the unweighted mean of every available station at least $R$ from the target, the target excluded, per day and component. It has no parameters and is run at $R = 0$, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400 and 600~km. D1 is the far stack at $R = 400$~km, the distance of \citet{bachelot2025}, computed separately; its results agree with those of the far stack at 400~km. \citet{bachelot2025} use the eight nearest stations beyond 400~km in a graph neural network and compare it with a stack of the same stations; D1 uses every station beyond 400~km.

\subsection{Leave-one-out filters over nearest neighbours}
\label{sec:filters-loo}

These filters predict the target from its $K$ nearest stations beyond $R$, using only day $t$ (M1--M3) or the neighbours' whole series (M4). Their settings were chosen on the validation years by reconstruction error.

M1 is the weighted mean $\hat r_i(t) = \sum_j w_{ij} a_j(t) r_j(t) / \sum_j w_{ij} a_j(t)$ over the 16 nearest stations, with $w_{ij} = \exp(-d_{ij}^2/2\ell^2)/\bar\sigma_j^2$, $a_j(t)$ the availability and $\bar\sigma_j$ the station's median formal uncertainty over the training years, per component. The length scale $\ell$ was chosen from 10~km to infinity (a plain mean): 50~km at $R = 0$ for 16 and 64 neighbours, and 100~km for 256. M1 with 64 and 256 neighbours forms the neighbour-count experiment.

M2 follows \citet{kreemer2021}: a neighbour's value on day $t$ is dropped if it differs from that neighbour's own 31-day running median by more than $k$ times its robust scale; the rest are combined by a weighted median with M1's weights, interpolated as in \citet{kreemer2021}. With fewer than three neighbours left it falls back to M1. The tuned values at $R = 0$ are $\ell = 200$~km and $k = 5$.

M3 is ridge regression on the three components of the 16 neighbours, an availability bit per neighbour and an intercept, in units of each station's noise scale. Missing neighbours are set to zero and the remaining values rescaled by $K$ divided by the number available, so that gaps do not bias the fit. Rows are replicated four times with neighbours dropped at random. M3a fits one weight vector per station and component (65 features; $\lambda = 0.1$ in units of the trace of the Gram matrix per feature); the 178 stations without enough training rows, the held-out stations among them, use M3b. M3b fits one weight vector per component for all stations, adding each neighbour slot's log distance and azimuth and their products with the slot's residuals (257 features; $\lambda = 0.01$).

M4 applies M3 separately to four period bands that add up exactly to each series (under 7, 7--60, 60--400 and over 400 days), obtained with differences of zero-phase Gaussian smoothers, and sums the four predictions; $\lambda$ is chosen per band. Before filtering, each series' gaps are filled by linear interpolation of its own days, and a neighbour missing on a day contributes zero with its availability bit off. The band filters read the neighbours' past and future days. M4a is per station and M4b pooled, as M3a and M3b.

\subsection{Learned filters}
\label{sec:filters-learned}

M3k and M5 are trained on quiet residuals: the residuals minus the earthquake offsets estimated in the training-years fit, on training days, outside the Ridgecrest exclusion and outside $\pm 30$ days of catalogued M $\ge$ 6 earthquakes, at stations that are not held out. The loss is the squared error in units of each station's robust noise scale. Training stops early on the validation reconstruction error, and each model was trained with five seeds; the reported results are those of seed 0.

M3k keeps M1's form and learns its kernel: $w_{ijc} = \mathrm{softplus}(f_\theta(g_{ij}, q_j))_c$, where $g_{ij}$ is the neighbour's log distance and the sine and cosine of its azimuth from the target, $q_j$ its log robust noise scale per component and record completeness, and $f_\theta$ a multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers of 32 units (1,411 parameters). The weights depend on where a neighbour is and what kind of station it is, never on its values, so once trained M3k is a fixed weighted mean of day $t$. It was trained over 16 and over 256 neighbours at every radius; at $R = 0$ the best epoch was 14 of 20 (16 neighbours) and 4 of 10 (256).

M5 is a space--time attention network. A sample is a target, its 32 nearest stations beyond $R$, chosen for each 64-day window among the 64 nearest by availability, and one summary node, the far stack. Each node has 17 input channels per day: residual, log formal uncertainty, availability, a flag for cells hidden by a training mask, the daily increment $r(t) - r(t-1)$ (zero unless both days exist), the station's log noise scale and record completeness, and the day of year (sine and cosine), all in units of the station's noise scale. A convolution cuts each node's window into 4-day tokens; three blocks then alternate attention over a node's own tokens (time) and attention over the nodes at the same token (space), the latter biased by each node's log distance, azimuth and type relative to the target ($d = 64$, four heads). The output is a correction added to the far stack, so training starts from the far stack. The target is hidden for the whole window, so predictions never read it. Training used batches of 64 samples, a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ with 500 warm-up steps, weight decay $10^{-4}$, dropout 0.1, neighbour dropout 0.3, and early stopping every 500 steps (best step 5,000 at $R = 0$). Prediction uses 64-day windows overlapping by half, combined with a triangular taper, so a day's prediction reads neighbours' days before and after it. M5 was trained at $R = 0$, 10, 25, 50 and 100~km.

M5-aug is the same network trained with planted transients. In half of the training samples a synthetic field is added to the inputs of every node and to the far-stack node as it would enter that mean, but not to the targets, so copying it into a prediction increases the loss. The field is centred at a random point within $R + 300$~km of the target, with a width of 5--150~km, an amplitude of 1--15 times the station's noise scale, a duration of 5--60 days with a raised-cosine rise and fall, and a random horizontal direction; half are Gaussian blobs and half dipoles, whose sign flips across a random line through the centre. The generator uses its own seed and shapes and runs on training days only, so it is separate from the evaluation transients. M5-aug was trained at $R = 0$ and 25~km (best step 1,500 at $R = 0$).

\label{sec:res-velocity}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ===========================================================================

This section and the next are the drafting tool's interpretation of the results, written so that the study can be read as a whole. They are one reading of the numbers, not the author's, and the paper's discussion is to be written independently.

\subsection{What sets the signal a filter removes}

The results are consistent with the hypothesis of the proposal: the fraction of a transient that a filter removes depends mainly on how much of the filter's input lies inside the transient. For a linear filter, Eq.~\eqref{eq:retention} gives $\rho = 1 - \langle P(s), s\rangle / \langle s, s \rangle$, so the loss is the part of the transient that the filter's own prediction reproduces. For the far stack at $R = 0$, the prediction is the network mean of $s$, and a Gaussian transient contributes to that mean in proportion to the share of stations it covers. Computing this share from the station positions alone, ignoring data gaps, gives median retentions of 1.00, 0.985, 0.95 and 0.85 for widths of 10, 25, 50 and 100~km, against measured values of 1.00, 0.98, 0.94 and 0.83. The far stack therefore loses a 100-km transient for a simple geometric reason: such a transient covers a noticeable fraction of a network that is itself only a few hundred kilometres across.

For the nearest-neighbour filters the same reasoning works the other way. The 16th neighbour lies at a median of 40~km, so a 25-km transient covers most of a station's neighbours and a 100-km transient covers all of them; M1 keeps 0.25 and 0.00 of these. Increasing the exclusion radius moves the neighbours outside the transient, and retention rises when $R$ becomes comparable with $2L$ to $3L$: M1 keeps 0.83 of 25-km transients at $R = 50$~km, 0.73 of 50-km transients at $R = 100$~km and 0.70 of 100-km transients at $R = 200$~km. The price is that the remaining neighbours are farther away and fewer stations share their noise with the target, so noise removed falls from 14.1\% to 4.7\%, and spurious signal rises, because a transient now reaches stations whose neighbours are inside it while they themselves are not.

The exclusion radius is therefore not a parameter with an optimum but a choice of which transients to protect. For the far stack, raising $R$ from 0 to 200~km raises retention of 100-km transients from 0.83 to 0.96 at a cost of 0.6 points of noise removed and 0.006 in reconstruction error; at 400~km, all transients are kept, but noise removed falls by 2.2 points and spurious signal doubles. Which point is preferable depends on the transients a study is looking for. The share of a transient that the far stack absorbs is not the whole cost: it reappears, with opposite sign, at every other station of the network (Table~\ref{tab:spurious-extent}), so the far stack's spurious signal is small at each station and network-wide.

The seasonal harmonic is the limiting case. It is present at every station with nearly the same amplitude and phase, so a filter that averages the network removes about nine tenths of it at the typical station (Section~\ref{sec:res-seasonal}), and the exclusion radius does not help, because the harmonic 400~km away is the same harmonic. This is the reason the audit removes the harmonic before filtering and the reason the removed function must be added back afterwards by anyone who wants it: it cannot pass through a network filter. The ridge filters that keep a quarter or more of it do so by shrinking their weights, which also costs them common-mode noise, not by telling the seasonal signal from the common mode.

\subsection{How many stations, and how far}

On the validation years the weighted mean of the 256 nearest stations and the far stack had the same reconstruction error, which suggested that the number of stations averaged, not their distance, is what reduces the error. The test years do not support that reading in its simple form: the far stack's error is lower by 0.04, and the 64-neighbour mean has a lower error than the 256-neighbour one. The two filters differ in more than the number of stations, since M1 weights its neighbours by distance with a length scale tuned on the validation years, and the comparison cannot separate the two effects. What holds in both periods is that averaging more stations keeps more signal (25-km retention 0.25, 0.53, 0.84 and 0.98 for 16, 64, 256 and all stations), and that no filter over a subset of nearby stations removed more noise than the mean over all of them on the test years. A mechanism that fits both periods is the noise of the estimate itself: the mean of 16 neighbours carries about a quarter of a station's own scatter into the prediction ($\sigma/\sqrt{16}$), and the error of a filter that averages 16 stations cannot fall below that; the mean of 1,250 stations carries almost none. This is a property of the number of stations averaged, not of their distance, and the gains from 16 to 64 to 256 to all stations follow it. Which stations to average is then a question of signal, not of noise.

\subsection{Local filters and the far field}

The fault-slip events show a pattern that the Gaussian transients cannot: the nearest-neighbour filters keep afterslip close to the fault and remove it farther away. Within 5~km of the patch, every filter keeps 0.94--1.01; at 15--40~km, M1 keeps 0.11 and M3k 0.13, while the far stack keeps 1.00. Near the fault the displacement varies over distances shorter than the spacing of the neighbours, and on a strike-slip fault the two sides move in opposite directions, so the neighbours' mean does not reproduce the motion at the target. Far from the fault the displacement field is smooth over the scale of the neighbourhood, and a neighbour average reproduces it, as it does for a wide Gaussian transient. The far field of afterslip and slow slip constrains the depth and moment of the slip, so a local filter removes the part of the signal that a source inversion relies on. The Gaussian transients are in this sense pessimistic near faults and optimistic far from them, and the whole-event retention, dominated by the near-fault stations with the largest motion (0.79--1.00 for every filter), hides the difference.

\subsection{Learned filters}

Learning helps a filter that is restricted to the same inputs: the learned kernel has a lower error than the fixed Gaussian kernel over 16 neighbours (0.595 against 0.621) and over 256 (0.562 against 0.606), and removes more noise, at the same or lower retention. None of the learned filters, however, has a reconstruction error distinguishable from that of the far stack on the test years, on seen or on held-out stations, and every one keeps less planted signal. The attention network is built as the far stack plus a learned correction; without planted transients in training, the correction removes part of every coherent transient (100-km retention 0.59 against 0.83) without a measurable reduction in error, and with them (M5-aug), it keeps signal almost as well as the far stack and removes the same noise. Its Gaussian retention is not independent evidence, because the augmentation adds Gaussian blobs and dipoles to the training inputs, the same family as the planted transients; the fault-slip events, which the augmentation does not contain, are the test that shows it has learned the principle rather than the shape (0.99 at 15--40~km and 0.97 beyond 40~km from the patch). Across three training seeds the retention of each model moves by at most 0.07 while the gap between them stays at 0.23 or more (Table~\ref{tab:seeds}), so the difference is one of training, not of seed. On the validation years, the learned kernel over 256 neighbours had a lower error than the far stack with an interval excluding zero; that this advantage disappeared on the test years suggests that part of what was learned was specific to the validation period, or that the test years, which contain a different mix of noise and earthquakes, do not reward it. On this network and with daily data, the far stack is the baseline the learned filters did not beat, and the measure that separates them from it is signal kept rather than noise removed.

\subsection{Target-inclusive filters and the measures}

The target-inclusive filters illustrate why scatter reduction alone misleads. M2-self is M2 with the target added to the median: it removes 19.3\% of the scatter against 12.2\% for M2, while keeping the same signal (0.24 against 0.28 at 25~km) and real offsets (0.14 against 0.12). The extra noise removed is part of the station's own noise, which a filter that reads the target can subtract and a leave-one-out filter cannot; it says nothing about the common mode. C1, the network mean with the target included, behaves like the far stack at $R = 0$ on every measure, since one station out of 1,251 contributes little to the mean. C3 shows a different cost: with one region and one component its spurious signal is 1.58 times the planted amplitude for 100-km transients, because a single component fitted to the whole network spreads part of any large transient over every station; more regions reduce the spurious signal but remove more of the transient. Velocity repeatability rewards the same behaviour as scatter reduction: M2-self has the smallest vertical $|\Delta v|$, and a filter that absorbs real changes in velocity improves this measure. It also shows the opposite failure: the far stack and D1 worsen the north value because they imprint the velocity change of the network mean, driven by postseismic motion on the north coast, on every station (Section~\ref{sec:res-velocity}). Reconstruction error did not predict retention either: M3k has a lower error than M1 and M2 and keeps as little. Retention and spurious signal are therefore not refinements of the noise measures but independent axes, and a comparison of filters that omits them can rank a filter that removes signal above one that does not. Spurious signal needs its extent as well as its peak: the far stack's ghost is small at each station and present at all of them, and the peak alone makes it look like M1's.

\subsection{Relation to Bachelot et al.}

The far stack at 400~km (D1) keeps every planted transient and all of the afterslip, consistent with the choice of \citet{bachelot2025} to exclude nearby stations, and the far stack performing as well as the learned filters here parallels their finding that a stack of distant stations performed comparably to their network. The audit adds what the fixed distance costs: compared with the far stack at $R = 0$, D1 removes 2.2 points less of the scatter and creates twice the spurious signal for 100-km transients, while the gain in retention is mostly for transients of 50~km and wider (0.94 to 1.00 at 50~km and 0.83 to 1.00 at 100~km, against 0.98 to 1.00 at 25~km); D1 also carries the largest velocity imprint of any filter here (2.50~mm/yr north), because the far set of a southern station is dominated by the northern stations with postseismic motion. On this network, a smaller radius keeps transients up to 50~km almost completely (0.96 at $R = 50$~km).

\subsection{Limitations}

The audit uses one dense network in a strike-slip setting; in a sparse network, such as Türkiye's, where the median distance to the nearest NGL station is about 60~km, every filter's neighbours are farther away and the trade-off shifts. The fault-slip events use an elastic half-space, uniform tapered slip and two templates. The real offsets have no ground truth, and on the test years two thirds of them come from one earthquake. Only daily solutions are used. The test years were looked at while the scoring rules were revised, and several comparisons differ between the validation and test years, so the prospective evaluation is needed before any of the smaller differences reported here can be relied on. Velocity repeatability is measured on the 590 stations without an earthquake step in the windows, while the filters average every station, including those with postseismic motion; the measure therefore mixes a filter's effect on noise with the transfer of real deformation between stations (Section~\ref{sec:res-velocity}). Noise removed is a fast-band measure, and the conclusions about it do not extend to the long-period common mode. Seasonal retention uses the harmonic fitted over 2008--2019 as if it were the station's true seasonal signal; it measures what the filter does to that function, not to the year-to-year variation of the real seasonal cycle, which stays in the residuals and is not scored separately.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
%% ===========================================================================

This section is the drafting tool's summary of its own interpretation (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The signal a common-mode filter removes is set by how much of its input lies inside the transient. Filters built on the 16 nearest stations remove most of any transient wider than about 25~km; the mean of all stations loses only the share of a transient that enters the network mean, 0.17 of a 100-km transient on this network, and that share reappears with opposite sign at every other station, so its spurious signal is small per station and network-wide.
  \item The exclusion radius trades signal kept against noise removed and spurious signal; there is no radius that is best on every measure, and the choice depends on the transients of interest.
  \item On realistic fault slip, local filters keep the near-fault signal and remove the far field, which Gaussian transients and whole-event averages do not show.
  \item On the test years, no learned filter reduced the reconstruction error below that of the far stack, and each kept less signal; training with planted transients brought the attention network's signal kept up to, not beyond, that of the far stack, consistently across three training seeds.
  \item Scatter reduction, velocity repeatability and reconstruction error all reward filters that remove signal or the station's own noise, and noise removed here is a fast-band measure; signal kept, spurious signal with its extent, and the velocity imprint of the common-mode estimate need to be reported alongside them. On this network the far stack and D1 imprint the north-coast postseismic motion on the velocities of every station. A station's seasonal harmonic, nearly the same across the network, does not survive any averaging filter (about 0.1 kept), so it has to be removed before filtering and added back after.
\end{enumerate}
